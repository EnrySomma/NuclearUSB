1. SHELTER PRIORITIZATION
In extreme weather, a shelter is your absolute first priority. Hypothermia can occur
quickly in wet or windy conditions even at mild temperatures.

2. THE DEBRIS HUT (PRIMITIVE INSULATED SHELTER)
The debris hut is one of the most effective wilderness shelters because it traps body
heat without requiring a fire.
- Find a sturdy ridgepole branch (about 1.5 times your height).
- Prop one end of the ridgepole onto a sturdy tree fork, stump, or A-frame support.
  The other end rests on the ground.
- Lean thick ribs (branches) along both sides of the ridgepole, creating an angled
  triangular structure. Leave a small entry hole just large enough to crawl in.
- Cover the ribs with a thick layer of fine debris (leaves, moss, pine needles, twigs).
  The debris layer should be at least 2 to 3 feet thick for proper insulation.
- Fill the inside of the hut with a thick bed of dry leaves or pine needles to insulate
  your body from the cold ground.

3. TARP SHELTERS
If you have a modern tarp or heavy-duty plastic sheeting:
- Lean-To: Tie a cord between two trees, drape the tarp over, and peg one side to the
  ground. Leaves the front open, ideal if you have a campfire reflecting heat.
- A-Frame: Tie a cord between two trees, drape the tarp over, and peg both sides to the
  ground. Provides protection from wind on both sides.

4. SNOW SHELTERS
In snowy environments, snow is an excellent insulator.
- Quinzhee: Pile snow into a large mound. Insert sticks 12 inches deep all around
  the mound to act as thickness guides. Let the snow harden (sinter) for 2 hours.
  Hollow out the inside until you reach the tips of the indicator sticks. Poke a small
  ventilation hole in the top.
- Snow Cave: Dig horizontally into a deep, solid snowdrift. Keep the sleeping platform
  higher than the entry tunnel to trap warm air.
